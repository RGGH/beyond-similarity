Scene 07 — Review & Zoom Out

Formula Query is a ranking function you write. LTR is one the data writes.

Stepping back from the mechanics to place this technique in the wider landscape of ranking.

01What Formula Query actually provides

Underneath the sliders and the decay curves, Formula Query is a general-purpose way of constructing a ranking function inside the database: an expression tree of sums, products, and decay curves over $score and payload fields, evaluated per-candidate at query time.

The additive popularity-plus-recency formula is a simple useful case. The same mechanism supports considerably more — nested expressions, multiple decay curves stacked on different fields, conditional terms — without leaving the query language.

02Beyond simple score boosting

It's tempting to think of this as "similarity plus a couple of bonus points," but the formula is really a small ranking DSL. Decay functions alone open up a fair amount of range — linear for a hard, predictable cutoff; exponential when a small amount of staleness should barely matter but a lot should matter a great deal; Gaussian for a soft window around an ideal value that isn't necessarily "as recent as possible" (think: shipping estimates, or price closeness to a target).

03Hand-designed ranking vs. learned ranking

Formula Query sits at one end of a spectrum. At the other end is Learn-to-Rank (LTR): instead of a person deciding that popularity deserves weight 0.2, a model is trained on historical interaction data — clicks, purchases, dwell time — to learn what actually predicts a good outcome, often across dozens of signals a person would never hand-weight individually.

Formula QueryLearn-to-Rank
You define the ranking function directly — the weights and shapes are choices you make.The ranking function is learned from data — weights (and interactions between signals) are fit by a model.
Transparent: every term's contribution is visible and explainable.Often opaque: a gradient-boosted tree or neural ranker doesn't hand you a clean explanation.
Needs no training data — works from day one on a new catalogue.Needs meaningful volumes of historical interaction data to learn from.
Cheap to change: adjust a weight, redeploy instantly.Expensive to change: retrain, validate, redeploy a model.

04When each approach makes sense

Reach for Formula Query when...

You're early, interaction data is thin, stakeholders need to understand and trust the ranking, or the business logic itself is genuinely simple enough to state as a formula.

Reach for LTR when...

You have real interaction volume, the number of useful signals has grown past what a person can weight by hand, and small ranking improvements are worth the cost of training and maintaining a model.

The two aren't mutually exclusive in practice — a common path is to ship a Formula Query ranking first, use it (and the interaction data it generates) to bootstrap a later LTR model, and keep the formula as a transparent fallback.
